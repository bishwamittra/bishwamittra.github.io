Foundations of Large Language Models

Abilities and Limitations of Large Language Models: A Formal Language Learning Perspective

Seminar Inspired by Michael Hahn's prior course: theory-25

The rise of large language models (LLMs) has drawn researchers from theory and empirical machine learning into a shared inquiry: what are the abilities and limitations of these models? A common thread across both perspectives is the use of formal languages to study LLMs — theoretically, to ask which languages or tasks are learnable, and empirically, to test whether these predictions hold in practice and to probe other phenomena of LLMs beyond learnability. The seminar is a chance to engage with LLMs from both angles — useful whether your interests lean toward formal methods or applied machine learning.

Our pursuit of understanding LLMs is divided into three axes:

  • No assumption about the computational model. What do classical results say about the learnability of a language, agnostic to any computational model?
  • A simplified model of the transformer. What is the computational model of a transformer? Can we study this model to understand the expressiveness and learnability of transformers?
  • LLMs in practice. Can we explain the phenomena of LLMs, e.g., different modes of learning, the world model implicit in the LLM, and the interplay between memorization and learning?

In each axis, controlled formal languages help us derive precise claims about the abilities and limitations of LLMs — something that is otherwise difficult with natural language.

Logistics

  • Schedule: Weekly, Thursday, 1–3 pm.
  • Intended audience: PhD and Master's students in computer science.

  • Registration: Register here.

Grading

  • Presentation (40%). A 45–60 minute presentation of the assigned paper, followed by Q&A. Motivate the question and state it formally, go deep on one proof or experiment and explain why you find it important, and explain your takeaways from the paper.
  • Report (40%). A report on the presented paper, to be submitted before the next class. Maximum 2 pages. It should discuss at least one aspect of the paper that an AI reviewer, the presenter, or the original authors have missed, and introduce insights to motivate future research. The best three reports will count toward the grade.
  • Class participation (20%). Based on engagement during the Q&A following each presentation. Read the paper before the session and ask questions — the more difficult, the better.

Students pass the seminar with a combined score of at least 50% across all three components.
